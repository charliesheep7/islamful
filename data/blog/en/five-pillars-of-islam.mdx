If you have ever wondered what actually makes someone a practicing Muslim, the answer is remarkably compact. Not a long list of rules — five acts. They cover what you believe, how you spend your day, what you do with your money, one month of your year, and one journey in your lifetime.

> **Quick Answer:** The five pillars of Islam are the shahada (declaration of faith), salah (five daily prayers), zakat (obligatory charity), sawm (fasting Ramadan), and hajj (pilgrimage to Mecca). They come from the Prophet's ﷺ statement: **"Islam is built upon five"** (Narrated by al-Bukhari, 8; Muslim, 16). Every sane adult Muslim is responsible for all five, though zakat and hajj depend on means.

The Arabic of that hadith is worth knowing, because it sets the image the whole religion is built on:

بُنِيَ الْإِسْلَامُ عَلَى خَمْسٍ

*Buniya al-Islamu ʿala khams*

"Islam is built upon five." (Narrated by al-Bukhari, 8; Muslim, 16)

A pillar holds up a structure. Remove one and the building does not simply lose a feature — it becomes unstable. That is the point of the metaphor.

**Key Points:**

| # | Pillar | What it requires | Who it applies to |
|---|---|---|---|
| 1 | Shahada | Testifying that there is no god but Allah and Muhammad ﷺ is His Messenger | Every Muslim, once sincerely and for life |
| 2 | Salah | Five prayers at fixed times each day | Every sane adult Muslim, no exemption |
| 3 | Zakat | 2.5% of qualifying wealth held for a lunar year | Those whose wealth reaches the nisab |
| 4 | Sawm | Fasting from dawn to sunset through Ramadan | Healthy adults; the ill and travelers make it up later |
| 5 | Hajj | Pilgrimage to Mecca | Once in a lifetime, for those physically and financially able |

The rest of this guide takes each pillar in turn: what it is, what it actually asks of you, and the evidence it rests on.

## What Is the Shahada, the First Pillar?

The shahada is a single sentence, and saying it sincerely is what makes a person a Muslim:

أَشْهَدُ أَنْ لَا إِلَٰهَ إِلَّا اللهُ وَأَشْهَدُ أَنَّ مُحَمَّدًا رَسُولُ اللهِ

*Ash-hadu an la ilaha illa Allah, wa ash-hadu anna Muhammadan rasul Allah*

"I bear witness that there is no god but Allah, and I bear witness that Muhammad is the Messenger of Allah."

It has two halves and both matter. The first half is **tawhid** — that worship belongs to Allah (SWT) alone, with no partner, no intermediary, no rival. The second half is **risalah** — that Muhammad (peace be upon him) is the one through whom that worship was taught, which is why Muslims follow his sunnah rather than inventing their own forms of devotion.

The shahada is not a password. It is a claim you are then expected to live by, which is exactly why the other four pillars exist. Someone who declares faith and then abandons prayer entirely has stated something their life contradicts.

Practically, this pillar is also the door in. A person entering Islam says the shahada — ideally in front of witnesses, though scholars agree that witnesses are recommended rather than strictly required. ## Why Is Salah the Second Pillar?

Salah is the five daily prayers: Fajr before sunrise, Dhuhr after midday, Asr in the afternoon, Maghrib just after sunset, and Isha at night. Allah commands it directly alongside zakat: **"And establish prayer and give zakah"** ([Surah Al-Baqarah, 2:43](https://quran.com/2/43)).

What makes salah different from the other pillars is its frequency. Hajj happens once. Ramadan comes annually. Zakat is calculated yearly. Salah interrupts you five times a day, every day, for your whole adult life — which is precisely the design. It is the pillar that refuses to let faith become an abstraction.

Two details trip people up:

- **The times are not flexible slots.** Each prayer has a defined window, and praying before that window opens does not count. This is why Muslims check local prayer times rather than praying on a fixed clock schedule — the windows shift daily with sunrise and sunset.
- **It is owed even when you are unwell or traveling.** The form can change (a traveler shortens four-rakah prayers to two; someone who cannot stand prays seated), but the obligation itself does not lift. Unlike fasting, there is no category of person who is simply excused.

## What Is Zakat and Who Has to Pay It?

Zakat is not a donation you choose to make. It is a fixed obligation on wealth that has sat with you for a full lunar year, and it belongs to specific categories of recipients named in the Quran.

The rate is **2.5%** — one quarter of one tenth — on qualifying savings, gold, silver, and trade goods. You only owe it if your wealth reaches the **nisab**, the minimum threshold, which is the value of 85 grams of gold or 595 grams of silver. Below that, you owe nothing. Scholars at [Islam Q&A explain the evidence for the 2.5% rate](https://islamqa.info/en/answers/145600) from the authentic sunnah in detail.

The word itself carries the meaning. *Zakat* comes from a root meaning to purify and to grow — which is how the Quran frames it: **"Take, [O Muhammad], from their wealth a charity by which you purify them and cause them increase thereby"** (Surah At-Tawbah, 9:103).

Three things people commonly get wrong:

1. **Zakat is not the same as sadaqah.** Sadaqah is voluntary charity, any amount, any time. Zakat is a calculated obligation with a deadline and a defined set of eligible recipients.
2. **The year is lunar, not solar.** A Hijri year is about eleven days shorter, so your zakat date drifts earlier each Gregorian year.
3. **Debts affect the calculation.** Money you genuinely owe and are actively repaying reduces the wealth zakat is assessed on.

Working out the actual figure is simpler than it sounds once you have the method — our guide on [how to calculate zakat](/blog/how-to-calculate-zakat) breaks the arithmetic down with worked examples.

## What Does Fasting Ramadan (Sawm) Require?

Sawm means abstaining from food, drink, and intimacy from the break of dawn until sunset, every day of Ramadan. The command is explicit, and so is its purpose:

**"O you who have believed, decreed upon you is fasting as it was decreed upon those before you that you may become righteous"** ([Surah Al-Baqarah, 2:183](https://quran.com/2/183)).

Note the last clause. The goal is not hunger — it is **taqwa**, God-consciousness. A fast where someone abstains from food but spends the day lying and losing their temper has missed the point entirely, and the Prophet ﷺ said as much about those who gain nothing from fasting but hunger and thirst.

This is the pillar with the clearest built-in exemptions:

- **The ill and travelers** postpone and make up the same number of days later.
- **Menstruating and postnatal women** do not fast during those days and make them up afterward.
- **The chronically ill and the very elderly**, who cannot reasonably expect to make days up, feed a needy person for each missed day instead.

None of these are loopholes. They are stated in the Quran itself, immediately after the verse commanding the fast.

Ramadan also carries practices beyond the fast — the night prayer of Taraweeh, increased Quran recitation, and the dua at the moment of breaking the fast — all built on the fast rather than replacing it.

## Who Must Perform Hajj?

Hajj is the pilgrimage to Mecca performed during the month of Dhul Hijjah, and unlike the other four pillars it is explicitly conditional:

**"And [due] to Allah from the people is a pilgrimage to the House — for whoever is able to find thereto a way"** ([Surah Aal-Imran, 3:97](https://quran.com/3/97)).

That phrase — "whoever is able" — is the whole ruling. Scholars call this condition **istitaʿah**, and it covers both physical capability and financial capability, including the ability to leave dependents provided for while you are away. Someone who cannot afford the journey without going into debt is not sinning by not going. They are simply not yet obliged.

Hajj is required **once in a lifetime**. Anything beyond that is voluntary. It is also distinct from Umrah, the lesser pilgrimage, which can be performed at any time of year and is not one of the five pillars.

What makes hajj unusual among the pillars is that it is the only one that dissolves social distinction so completely. Every pilgrim wears the same two undyed cloths, stands in the same place, and says the same words. Wealth, nationality, and status become invisible for several days — a rehearsal, in a sense, for standing before Allah with none of it.

## How Do the Five Pillars Fit Into Daily Life?

Read as a list, the five pillars can look like five separate obligations. Lived, they work as one system covering different time scales.

Salah structures your **day**. Zakat audits your **year** financially. Sawm reshapes one **month** annually. Hajj marks your **lifetime**. And the shahada runs underneath all four as the reason any of it means anything.

The practical advice scholars give beginners is consistent: build in order, and do not skip to the dramatic ones. Someone who has not established salah but is planning hajj has the structure backward. The Prophet ﷺ described the same order himself in the famous hadith of Jibril, where he defined Islam as testifying, praying, giving zakat, fasting Ramadan, and performing hajj for those able (Narrated by Muslim, 8).

Start where the load is lightest and the frequency is highest:

1. **Say and understand the shahada** — not just the words but both halves of what they claim.
2. **Establish one prayer**, then add the rest. Consistency beats volume.
3. **Check your wealth once a year** against the nisab. Many people discover they owe nothing yet.
4. **Fast the next Ramadan** properly, including the exemptions if they apply to you.
5. **Plan hajj when the means genuinely exist** — not before.

## Summary

The five pillars of Islam are the framework the entire religion is built on:

- **Shahada** — testifying that there is no god but Allah and Muhammad ﷺ is His Messenger. Required of every Muslim.
- **Salah** — five daily prayers at fixed times. No exemption for a sane adult Muslim; only the form adapts.
- **Zakat** — 2.5% of qualifying wealth held a lunar year, owed only above the nisab threshold.
- **Sawm** — fasting Ramadan from dawn to sunset, with stated exemptions for illness, travel, and genuine inability.
- **Hajj** — pilgrimage to Mecca once in a lifetime, obligatory only for those with the means.

All five appear together in one hadith: "Islam is built upon five" (Narrated by al-Bukhari, 8; Muslim, 16), and each is commanded individually in the Quran. If you are starting out, [Islam Q&A's summary of the pillars](https://islamqa.info/en/answers/13569) is a useful second reference alongside this guide.

Build them in order, keep the one that repeats most often, and the rest becomes far less daunting than the list suggests.

And Allah knows best. (والله أعلم)
