**Bleeding can break wudu according to some schools, but ordinary external bleeding does not invalidate it in others.** The location of the bleeding, whether it flows, and the school you follow matter. A cut finger, a nosebleed and menstrual bleeding should not all be put into one category.

This answer covers blood from an external wound, nose or gums. It does not decide the separate rules for menstruation, postnatal bleeding or discharge from the private parts.

## The main differences

| School or cited position                   | Ordinary external bleeding                                                                                              |
| ------------------------------------------ | ----------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------------- |
| Hanafi                                     | Blood flowing beyond the place it exits breaks wudu; a spot that stays at its exit point does not meet that rule.       |
| Maliki                                     | External bleeding itself does not break wudu.                                                                           |
| Shafi‘i                                    | External bleeding itself does not break wudu.                                                                           |
| Hanbali position summarized by Dar al-Ifta | Small amounts do not break wudu; larger amounts are distinguished. Ask a teacher about applying the amount distinction. |

The Hanafi flow rule is explained by [Darul Ifta Birmingham](https://daruliftabirmingham.co.uk/what-is-meant-by-flowing-blood-2/). The Maliki, Shafi‘i and Hanbali distinctions above come from [Dar al-Ifta’s discussion of nosebleeds](https://dar-alifta.org/ar/Fatwa/Details/18044/%D8%AD%D9%83%D9%85-%D8%A7%D9%84%D9%88%D8%B6%D9%88%D8%A1-%D9%85%D9%86-%D8%A7%D9%84%D8%AF%D9%85-%D8%A7%D9%84%D8%AE%D8%A7%D8%B1%D8%AC-%D9%85%D9%86-%D8%A7%D9%84%D8%A3%D9%86%D9%81). This is an overview of the cited positions, not a claim that every scholar gives an identical answer.

## A nosebleed or a cut while preparing for prayer

Dar al-Ifta’s [English answer on bleeding and ablution](https://dar-alifta.org/en/fatwa/details/7854/bleeding-and-ablution) adopts the view that a nosebleed or wound does not itself invalidate wudu, while recommending renewal in view of the disagreement. A recommendation to renew is different from saying that everyone’s previous wudu was invalid.

For a Hanafi reader, the practical question is whether the blood flowed beyond its point of exit. For a reader following the Maliki or Shafi‘i position described above, the external bleeding itself is not the nullifier. Avoid judging another person’s prayer from a different school’s rule without understanding their practice.

## Pimples, scratches and pus

Popping a pimple is not, by itself, the test. Under the cited Hanafi ruling, blood or pus that flows beyond the wound invalidates wudu. That includes discharge that would have flowed but was immediately wiped or absorbed by a dressing. A trace remaining at the exit point is different.

For example, a small visible dot and a discharge that spreads onto surrounding skin are different situations under that rule. This example illustrates the source’s distinction; it is not a new measurement or a reason to squeeze a spot to test it. See the [explanation of flowing blood and pus](https://daruliftabirmingham.co.uk/what-is-meant-by-flowing-blood-2/).

## What about bleeding gums?

Gum bleeding needs its own detail because blood mixes with saliva. [Darul Ifta Birmingham’s specific answer](https://daruliftabirmingham.co.uk/home/during-wudu-if-a-persons-gums-start-bleeding-do-they-have-to-wait-till-the-bleeding-stops/) says that, in the ruling it presents, blood predominating over saliva invalidates wudu; saliva predominating does not. Its answer uses the appearance of the spit to explain the distinction.

Do not substitute the separate rules about swallowing blood during fasting for this wudu question. If gum bleeding happens repeatedly, describe that recurring situation to a qualified teacher rather than treating every mouth rinse as a new isolated case. A dentist can assess the bleeding itself.

## A useful next step

Keep two questions separate when asking for help: “Does this invalidate my wudu under the school I follow?” and “How should I clean or protect this wound?” This page answers the first at an introductory level; it is not a wound-care guide or a ruling for continuous bleeding.
